**Against Certifiable Updating**

The argument starts from a theorem about programs and ends with a claim about rational credence. To get from one to the other it needs a principle linking what a norm requires to what an agent can establish about its own representation. That principle is where the argument fails, and the parts that survive are already familiar.

Consider first what the principle asks for. A computable agent holding the prior presented by the halting-indexed mixture can in fact update correctly. Given that the machine never halts, the constant kernel is the correct posterior, and a short program outputs it. The agent fails only to *know* that this program is correct. The principle therefore cannot be an ought-implies-can principle. It is an ought-implies-can-know principle. Principles of that kind are known to be too strong. An agent cannot know, for every sentence it believes, whether that sentence is consistent with the rest. It cannot know whether the program it uses to represent its prior is total. It cannot know whether its credences are coherent across an infinite algebra. If being bound by a norm required being able to verify that one meets it, almost no norm of Bayesian rationality would bind any computable agent. The argument singles out conditionalization only because the relevant failure there has a pleasing theorem attached.

The appeal to rule-following does not rescue the principle. On standard accounts, following a rule needs a disposition, guided by the rule, to produce the right outputs. It does not need a proof that the outputs are right. A child follows the rule for addition without being able to prove that her algorithm computes the sum. The agent whose program happens to be the constant kernel follows conditionalization in every sense that matters for the rule-following literature. What it lacks is a reflective guarantee, and reflective guarantees are not part of what rule-following is.

Second, representation-dependence is not new and not alarming. Since Hacking and Garber we have known that agents with logically equivalent beliefs can differ in what they are able to infer. Two agents can believe the same proposition under different sentences and still differ in whether they can draw a given consequence. If rational standing depends on that, then it has always depended on representation, and nothing specific to probability or to computability has been shown. If rational standing does not depend on that, then it does not depend on the program text either. Either way the new result adds an instance, not a lesson.

Third, the move to noise is a non sequitur. The certifiable class the argument favours is the class of priors with a bounded computable likelihood density. Membership in that class is certified only because the presentation includes the bound. That is a feature of the format, not of noise. Any class can be made certifiable by building a certificate into the format. A format could, for instance, carry an explicit computable disintegration alongside the prior, and that would certify updating for priors with exact evidence just as well. So the argument does not show that certifiability requires a channel model. It shows that certifiability requires certificates, which is trivial.

Fourth, even granting that a channel model is the convenient certificate, the reason for adopting it is of the wrong kind. Whether my thermometer adds noise to the temperature is a question about the thermometer. A reason that comes from the computational convenience of my own updating bears on it no more than my desire to feel calm bears on whether the bridge is safe. The proposal must either show that computability considerations are evidence about channels, which seems hopeless, or admit that it recommends adopting credences for practical reasons. That is something orthodox Bayesians, and most of their critics, reject.

Fifth, the proposal imposes a structural cost it does not price. In the bad cases the exact conditional is discontinuous everywhere. So the posterior under a channel model depends on the noise scale in a way that does not settle as the scale shrinks. Two agents who agree on everything about the world, and differ only in an arbitrarily small parameter of their channel model, can then reach posteriors that differ substantially, with no fact to adjudicate. The proposal calls this a mild idealization. It is better described as replacing one unanswerable question (what is the exact posterior?) with another (which noise model is correct?), with no guarantee that the second has an answer either.

What survives is the modest observation that some exact-evidence ideals cannot be implemented with a guarantee. That is true, and it is the standard gap between rational ideals and their algorithmic realization. It does not show that rational standing depends on representation, and it does not show that rational agents must doubt their evidence.
