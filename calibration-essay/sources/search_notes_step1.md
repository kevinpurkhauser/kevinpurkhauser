## SEP, Reliabilist Epistemology (sec. 5.3 in Fall 2019 edition, "Reliabilism and Degrees of Belief")
- Snippet: "According to the calibration approach, a credence is justified iff it is produced by a well-calibrated process. This avoids the objection to using the Brier score: after all, a credence-forming process that produces mid-level credences can still be well-calibrated."
- Snippet: "Given a particular scoring rule R, we can develop a measure of process reliability (Dunn 2015; Tang forthcoming)."
- Snippet (Goldman 1986 case as reported by SEP): agent A, 70% of whose opinions are true, "can achieve a perfectly calibrated credence function by adopting a .7 credence in every proposition about which she has an opinion"; Goldman: wrong to conclude automatically that A's credal state is perfectly reliable; "if A has no good reason for adopting a .7 credence in many of the propositions in question, then her credal state shouldn't count as justified."
- Snippet: "Dunn (2015) defends the calibration approach, arguing that the relevant question is whether the process that produced the credal state is reliable."
- Snippet: "Tang (2016) objects to the calibration approach on the grounds that a credence-forming process can be well-calibrated even though that process is insensitive to relevant evidence."
- Snippet (Tang's view as reported): "A credence of C(p) is only justified if it is based on some ground g, such that the objective probability of the credence having a true content given g approximates C(p)."
- Snippet (Pettigrew as reported by SEP): "we can develop a version of the calibration approach that makes reference to the agent's evidence. Once we do, this version turns out to be extensionally equivalent to a version of Tang's approach."
- No snippet showed Foster, Vohra, Sandroni, Dawid or Belot cited in the SEP entry.

## Dunn (2015), Philosophical Studies 172(7): 1929-52, doi 10.1007/s11098-014-0380-2
- Abstract snippet: "When it comes to degrees of belief, reliability and truth-possession part ways. The natural answer thus fails to be a good way to evaluate degrees of belief for reliability. I propose and develop an alternative method based on the notion of calibration, suggested by Frank Ramsey."
- Footnote snippet: "A process is well-calibrated if it produces beliefs in P to degree n and n % of the propositions like P are true."
- Text snippet: "We should not merely look at the actual cases that these processes have performed on. We must also look at how they perform in nearby worlds."
- Secondary (Wilcox 2024, Rev. Phil. Psych., "Credences and Trustworthiness: a Calibrationist Account"): Dunn holds "the reliability of such a process depends on how well calibrated its credences are in actual and nearby counterfactual scenarios."
- Secondary: Dunn "says little about which (hypothetical) counter-factual circumstances are relevant. He merely says that 'it's a difficult question in general'"; hypothetical circumstances "may differ from actual ones only in the accidental features of the agent's environment."

## Tang (2016), Mind 125(497): 63-94, doi 10.1093/mind/fzv199
- Abstract (via PhilArchive/OUP listing): reliabilism as it stands cannot account for justified credence; examines three ways the reliabilist may try; finds problems with each; defends a hybrid view incorporating evidentialist elements.
- Classified by Pettigrew (2021) and others as "indicator reliabilism"; Dunn as "process reliabilism".

## Pettigrew (2021), Episteme 18(1): 16-30, doi 10.1017/epi.2018.50
- Abstract: Dunn (2015) proposes process reliabilism, Tang (2016) indicator reliabilism; Pettigrew argues they are extensionally equivalent; both face the "Connection Problem" (unclear how justification connects to epistemic value); offers a version that is "both process and indicator" and argues it solves the problem. Keywords include accuracy and calibration.
- Full text not readable here; details of his positive account NOT verified.

## Belot (2013), "Failure of calibration is typical", Statistics and Probability Letters 83: 2316-2318 (arXiv 1306.4943)
- Abstract (as summarized): builds on Schervish, who showed every forecasting system is uncalibrated for uncountably many data sequences; Belot strengthens this to show calibration failure is topologically typical (failure is residual / calibration is meagre); notes Bayesian forecasters are certain they will be calibrated, raising questions about Bayesianism and rationality.
- Snippet: cites Foster and Vohra's randomized result and says, in his setting, mixing strategies "In itself, this will make no difference" (context not fully verified).

## Seidenfeld (1985), "Calibration, coherence, and scoring rules", Philosophy of Science 52(2): 274-294
- Abstract: "Can there be good reasons for judging one set of probabilistic assertions more reliable than a second?" Conclusions: (i) calibration surveys useless without considering interconnections between questions; (ii) "Subject to feedback, calibration in the long run is otiose. It gives no ground for validating one coherent opinion over another"; (iii) "Calibration in the short run is an inducement to hedge forecasts. A calibration score, in the short run, is improper."

## Dawid (1982), "The well-calibrated Bayesian", JASA 77: 605-610
- Abstract: "We prove a theorem to the effect that a coherent Bayesian expects to be well calibrated." (probability one under own prior). Oakes (1985, JASA) shows no forecasting system is calibrated for every sequence (self-calibrating priors do not exist).

## Isaacs (2021), "The fallacy of calibrationism", PPR (doi 10.1111/phpr.12640)
- Abstract snippet: argues calibrationism is mistaken since it relies on the base-rate fallacy. Note: "calibrationism" here is the higher-order-evidence view (align credence with estimated reliability), a different view from calibrationist reliabilism.

## Ye (2023), Higher-Order Evidence and Calibrationism (Cambridge Elements)
- Calibrationism = respond to higher-order evidence by aligning credences to one's reliability degree. Different target (same word).

## Expert-testing side (no philosophy-of-justification paper found linking it to reliabilism)
- Foster and Vohra (1998) Biometrika: randomized forecaster calibrated against any sequence.
- Sandroni (2003) IJGT: any test that the true expert passes with high probability can be passed by an uninformed strategic expert with high probability, whatever the outcomes.
- Olszewski (handbook chapter "Calibration and expert testing", Handbook of Game Theory vol. 4, 2015): argues Popperian falsifiability cannot separate scientific theories from worthless ones when theories are produced by strategic experts.
- Non-manipulable tests: Dekel and Feinberg (2006), Shmaya (2008), Olszewski and Sandroni (2009) (per arXiv 1405.7481 summary).

## Additional snippets (second round)
- SEP passage on nearby worlds in the Goldman/Dunn exchange: "It's not enough to look at the truth-ratio of A's opinions at the actual world; rather, we should look across a range of nearby worlds. If it's just a matter of chance that 70% of the propositions A has an opinion about are true, then the calibration approach will be able to avoid the counterintuitive consequence." (Attribution inside SEP between Goldman's remedy and Dunn's reply is not fully clear from the snippet; I treat it as SEP's statement of the calibrationist's nearby-worlds reply.)
- Dunn (2015) text snippet: "there has been considerable debate about whether or not the truth-ratio for a process should be calculated based on the set of beliefs produced by that process in the actual world, in all possible worlds, or in some special subset of the possible worlds."
- Wilcox (2024), Review of Philosophy and Psychology 16(1): 305-344, "Credences and Trustworthiness: a Calibrationist Account": "such judgments are trustworthy only to the extent that one has good reasons to think that they are produced by maximally inclusive, well calibrated cognitive processes"; inclusive = takes into account all evidence the agent considers relevant; calibrated = e.g. 80% assignments true about 80% of the time. ("inclusive calibrationism")
- Williams and Pettigrew, "Consequences of Calibration", BJPS (doi 10.1086/725097): calibration test as constraint on accuracy measures; any additive continuous accuracy measure passing it is strictly proper. Different question (accuracy measurement, not justification).
- Levinstein, "Accuracy Uncomposed: Against Calibrationism", Episteme: critiques Pettigrew's Decomposition axiom (inaccuracy evaluated partly by calibration). Different question.
- Dorst (2023), "Being Rational and Being Wrong", Philosophers' Imprint 23, doi 10.3998/phimp.597: rational agents can be expected to be miscalibrated; deference. Different question (calibration as evidence of irrationality), but relevant in showing calibration and rationality come apart in the other direction.
- Schoenfield, "A Dilemma for Calibrationism" (PPR): higher-order-evidence calibrationism; different view.
- Hedden (2021), "On statistical criteria of algorithmic fairness", PPA 49(2): 209-231: calibration within groups the one criterion necessary for fairness. Different question.
- Philosophical Studies 2025 (doi 10.1007/s11098-025-02322-y), "Does calibration mean what they say it means; or, the reference class problem rises again": reference-class problem for calibration and multicalibration. Relevant to Step 3 (multicalibration), not to the Foster-Vohra argument.
- Foster and Hart (2023), "Calibeating", Theoretical Economics (doi 10.3982/TE5330): snippet "Experts should better be tested by the Brier score and not by calibration alone."
- Al-Najjar and Weinstein (comparative testing): either the test identifies the informed expert or the uninformed expert's forecasts end up close to the informed one's (as summarized by search; to be checked in Step 2).
